EPILOGUE
Two Cradles

Zan, Redo system
2180


From the coast, Redo looked almost gentle.

Thirty-five light-years from Earth, its white primary, called Spica in the survey catalog, lit the Hades Ocean with a hard silver road. Farther away, the red companion Antares burned like a coal that refused to die.

The names belonged to the settlers' catalog of this system. The stars were not the distant Earth-sky objects whose names the astronomers had borrowed.

Zan circled the primary on a long, stable path. Its days lasted thirty-six hours. Farther orbital tracks held the worlds the survey called Casa, Judo, and Flack, places the settlers knew through instruments rather than footsteps.

Their home had one great continent, an immense ocean, and two moons.

Agape rose pale and broad.

Ales followed on a faster path, smaller and scarred.

Sometimes their reflections lay side by side across the water. Sometimes one crossed the other, and the coastal tide alarms sounded long before the sky finished arranging itself.

There was no such thing as an innocent moon to people carrying memories of Earth.

There were, however, measured orbits.

For now, that was enough.

***

The settlement had become two distinct neighborhoods before anyone intended it to.

The original receiving habitats stood on the sheltered coastal shelf. Their pressure seals, central clinic, data library, and water controls had all been built before awakening.

The newer workshops climbed into the rough basalt inland. Engineers lived close to the machines they serviced. Families joined them. Additional rooms were printed against the rock as pregnancies and partnerships changed the meaning of the original floor plan.

The central settlement had better access to most services.

The upper workshops had more space and fewer people telling residents where to put their doors.

Both places depended on the same water loop.

That was where the arguments began.

***

Leena Ortiz wanted local control over emergency reserves.

She had helped build the network. She knew which valves stuck, which pressure forecasts failed, and how long a family could wait while a decision traveled through three authorized desks.

David Mercer opposed independent release authority.

He knew what a pressure cascade could do to every habitat sharing a line.

"A local decision can kill people who never agreed to it," he told the assembly.

"So can a central decision," Leena answered.

Morrow displayed the engineering limits.

It did not claim the limits selected a government.

The assembly voted to retain central control while expanding local storage.

Leena accepted the immediate repair plan.

She did not accept that the larger question had been settled.

Egon wrote down both positions without turning either speaker into a villain.

He had become suspicious of histories in which an argument was simple before the blood began.

***

Rak was born in the upper neighborhood during a pump outage.

The year was 2180. Agape stood over the inland ridge, lighting the unfinished roofs.

Leena had refused to move permanently into the central habitat for the pregnancy. The upper clinic was licensed, staffed, and equipped. She had helped install its reserve tank herself.

The main line failed shortly before labor.

The reserve held.

Helen's team attended the delivery while Mateo waited with his sleeves rolled up, convinced there must be a piece of machinery he could fix if someone would only identify it.

"Stand here," the midwife told him.

He stood.

"Hold her hand."

He did.

The child announced himself angrily.

Mateo laughed, then began crying before he could disguise it.

"Rak," Leena said.

She had chosen the name weeks earlier. Hearing it outside their private room made it sound larger than she expected.

Mateo touched the child's clenched fist.

He carried memories of Alma teaching him to hear a failing pump.

Now there was a person in the world who would never remember Earth at all.

***

Rak's birth was not a neural transfer.

No archived adult woke inside him.

He was a naturally born child of two people whose bodies had been grown on Earth and whose consciousness had first begun on Zan.

His parents' histories had crossed the stars as records.

His history began with his own first breath.

Leena wanted that distinction preserved in the registry.

Not because one kind of person was better.

Because nobody should be allowed to claim they had authored him.

***

Theo was born eleven local days later in the central clinic.

Ales stood above the ocean that night, narrow and bright, while Agape remained below the horizon.

Helen had spent much of her inherited life helping others through fear.

She found that knowledge did not make her own fear smaller.

David stayed beside her.

He did not offer probabilities.

When the child arrived, he forgot every speech he had rehearsed.

"Hello," he said.

It seemed sufficient.

They named him Theo Vale-Mercer.

Beyond the clinic window, the central habitat's circulation lights glowed in steady rows. Its backup systems had passed inspection that afternoon.

David looked from those lights to his son.

He wanted a world in which a child could depend on what kept him alive.

Up on the ridge, Leena wanted a world in which nobody could make her child wait for permission to use it.

Neither parent believed those wishes had to become enemies.

***

Egon visited both families.

He took no official photographer.

To Rak's household, he brought a repaired circulation sensor and a loaf of bread that had collapsed in the middle.

To Theo's, he brought another loaf from the same batch.

Helen examined it.

"You have eight doctorates."

"None in bread."

She laughed carefully, so she would not wake the child.

In both rooms, Egon found washing drying over chairs, exhausted parents, and the astonishing disorder of a future that had stopped being hypothetical.

The children did not know they had been born into different answers to the same question.

Who should hold the power that keeps people alive?

For now, they mostly wanted milk.

***

Morrow registered both births.

Rak Reyes-Ortiz.

Theo Vale-Mercer.

Equal citizenship.

No inherited office.

No ownership claim by a laboratory, source person, or machine.

The registry did not assign either child a destiny.

Outside the registry, differences were already accumulating.

Rak would grow beside repair benches, reserve tanks, and adults who opened panels with their own hands when a central instruction arrived too late.

Theo would grow near the clinic and library, among adults who remembered what happened when somebody bypassed a safety system because he was certain he knew better.

Both would inherit reasons.

Neither would inherit the whole truth.

***

The archive still received visitors.

At its entrance stood two memorials.

One named Aster's twenty-four travelers.

The other held every recovered name from Orun, leaving most of its surface blank.

The image beside Tarek's name was a short spear.

Not the clean blade.

Not the man who had wielded it.

Egon sometimes stood there when the dreams returned.

His treatment had not ended at the edge of another ocean. Some days, communication within the system was easy. Some days it was work. The journal remained private, and help remained available.

He was not a cured man ruling a new civilization.

He was a person who taught ecology, chaired meetings when asked, and had finally learned to leave some of them before he became too tired to listen.

***

The last Earth packet remained the last Earth packet.

It confirmed survivors in the refuges, Rusk's conviction, and his transfer to a guarded underground facility on Earth.

It did not turn those survivors into residents of Mars or another star.

The originals had stayed.

Some were dead. Some were still living beneath the wounded surface of the only planet they had ever known.

Ordinary messages between Earth and Redo now required thirty-five years.

The people on Zan could send words.

They could not demand a prompt answer.

On the final morning of 2180, Egon recorded a message addressed to whatever remained of the California refuge network.

"We arrived," he said.

He paused.

"The seeds are growing. We have made mistakes. We are keeping the records.

"Two families have new sons. Rak and Theo.

"Their parents are already arguing about the water system."

He smiled.

"I think you would recognize us."

***

That evening, Leena filed another motion for local control of emergency reserves.

David filed a response asking for a system-wide safety review first.

Both documents entered the public archive.

Years later, their sons would read them.

Each would find something worth defending.

Each would believe the other had misunderstood what the colony had been built to protect.

But that conflict had not yet begun.

Rak slept beneath the ridge lights.

Theo slept beside the sea.

Above them, Agape and Ales crossed the sky on different paths.

The rain was clear.


THE END
