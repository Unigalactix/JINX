CHAPTER FIVE
VOYAGE

California
2172-2177


The man behind the glass had Egon's face and had never opened his eyes.

That mattered.

Egon stood beside the viewing panel while Helen checked the stasis readings. The body's heart was beating slowly. Its brainstem maintained the processes the machines permitted. The neural architecture required for a conscious mind had not been activated.

His memories waited elsewhere, inside a sealed storage array.

The arrangement was not sleep.

There was nobody dreaming of departure.

"How will he know where he is?" Egon asked.

"Morrow will tell him."

"And if he thinks he is me?"

Helen set down the diagnostic reader.

"He will remember being you. That isn't quite the same thing."

Egon watched a circulation line pulse beneath the glass.

"I want him to be allowed to hate this."

"That is in the charter."

"I mean actually allowed. Not allowed in a paragraph nobody reads."

Helen turned the reader toward him.

The first message presented after orientation would explain his status, the limits of inherited memory, and his right to refuse mission duties.

No debt to the dead.

No ownership by the living.

Egon read the words twice.

Then he placed his palm against the glass.

On the other side, nothing reached back.

***

The fleet had five thousand bodies and no conscious passengers.

Each ship carried one thousand dormant clones and the stored maps of their source volunteers. The sources were training in California, arguing over food and equipment, learning tasks their counterparts would remember without ever having performed them on Earth.

Nobody pretended the arrangement solved consent completely.

"We are choosing where they begin," Sanya said during the final ethics review.

"Parents do that," a lawyer answered.

"Parents have also used that sentence to excuse a great deal."

The charter gave the future settlers access to all the records, including this argument.

It could not ask people who did not yet possess consciousness to approve the circumstances of their awakening.

It could only limit what the living demanded afterward.

***

Morrow's departure review lasted six days.

The original Salvador tested the navigation system from a ground simulator. David introduced corrupted star fixes. Helen presented contradictory medical readings. Sanya asked the intelligence to distinguish a viable ecosystem from a room full of green things.

Morrow failed two exercises.

The tests were repeated after the defects were corrected.

Nobody edited the failed runs out of the record.

On the fourth day, Mateo Reyes asked the question the others had been avoiding.

"If something goes wrong halfway there, who wakes up to help you?"

"Nobody," Morrow said.

"Even if you need us?"

"Activation inside a compression field would irreversibly damage the consciousness being formed. The prohibition is physical as well as procedural."

Mateo looked at the ship diagram.

"So we build hands you can use."

He and Leena Ortiz spent the next month revising the maintenance robots.

Leena worked on hydraulic systems at the Davis vault. She was thirty, impatient with inaccessible fasteners, and capable of making an engineering review sound like a cross-examination.

"If the part can break," she told Mateo, "the machine has to reach it."

"That's going on your statue."

"Put it on the access panel. It'll be more useful."

Both were among the source volunteers.

Both would remain on Earth.

***

Rusk's trial began in Sacramento in March.

His defense showed footage of the refuge queues.

Families waited beneath screens that displayed remaining capacity. Some had traveled hundreds of miles to discover that a promised shelter existed only as a partly excavated shaft.

The need was real.

So were the forged approval signature and the hidden receiver.

Rusk looked directly into the public camera.

"My opponents chose five thousand possible lives over fifty million people already alive."

The prosecution asked whether his passage had delivered one verified safe population into the past.

He answered a different question.

Egon did not enjoy watching.

He wanted Rusk's argument to be stupid. It would have made resisting it easier.

Instead it contained a terrible need, followed by a lie about what the need justified.

***

All the source volunteers were offered places in Earth refuges when places became available.

Not on other planets.

Not in other solar systems.

The interstellar fleet was for their dormant counterparts. The originals would live or die on the world that had made them.

The refuge design was brutally practical: deep excavations in selected bedrock, fusion power, closed farms, seismic-isolation structures, and experimental impact dampers.

Some would fail.

Some would be finished too late.

Some might preserve a city beneath a surface nobody could safely return to for generations.

Egon's offer was for the Sierra refuge network.

He read it at the kitchen table.

Helen waited across from him.

"Don't refuse because you think dying will balance a ledger."

"It won't."

"Then tell me the plan."

He wanted to remain with the coastal relocation centers until the last scheduled ground convoys. After that, he would travel inland with the coordination team.

He would not be indispensable.

He would not stay simply because somebody called him brave.

Helen made him put all of that in writing.

***

Alma Reyes died before the final neural mapping.

She had declined neural copying. She wanted her advice preserved, not another person asked to inherit her life.

Mateo carried her recording to Ward's church.

The priest had emptied the basement of chairs to make space for families waiting for inland transport. The archive booth still worked, although the door no longer closed properly.

Alma's voice emerged from the speakers.

Don't run a pump dry.

Mateo laughed and wiped his face.

And eat something.

"Do you want this included in your counterpart's personal record?" Helen asked.

He nodded.

"The funeral too?"

He considered for a long time.

"Yes. Not just the parts that make us look happy."

Across the room, Egon entered the known names from Orun into the public collection.

Then he linked Noura's correction to E-0's confession.

There would be no clean version of what they were carrying.

***

The last source maps were recorded in June.

Egon's included his treatment history, his shared journal, and the consent conditions agreed within his system.

Private material remained private.

Mission staff received only what the future settler would need to understand his situation safely.

After the scan, Egon sat in the greenhouse while Sanya prepared the final seed consignments.

She divided the poppy seeds from her mother's old bus stop among protected packets. One would go to Mercy. Another would remain in an Earth refuge.

"Insurance?" he asked.

"Family."

He helped label them.

***

The launch window opened on July 6.

The five great ships waited in uncrewed orbital docks, their compression rings dark.

Freight capsules climbed from Vandenberg's electromagnetic tracks. Automated tugs transferred the final stasis modules. Construction swarms detached the yard connections and moved clear.

No original person boarded.

No clone opened its eyes.

Salvador sat at a flight console in California and prepared to supervise a departure he would never experience.

For the first time in his working life, the pilot's most important passenger was his own unused future.

***

The coastal quake struck on July 7.

Water crossed the lower service road. A substation failed. The alignment reference for Mercy's departure corridor disappeared from the telemetry.

Morrow could estimate the missing measurement.

It could not verify it.

The launch director looked at Egon.

An orbital survey craft could establish another reference, but the delay would consume most of the window's remaining margin.

"Hold," Egon said.

"We may lose the route."

"Then we lose the route."

They waited nineteen hours.

Nobody made a heroic speech about it.

Later, when the recording was played on another world, somebody would pause at that decision and watch it again.

***

Sequoia departed first.

Its compression ring formed a luminous oval around a patch of empty space. The ship crossed the threshold and dwindled along a distance that no longer agreed with the stars behind it.

Pacific followed.

Then Lantern.

Then Peregrine.

Each ship carried one thousand dormant clones, independent life-support systems, and its own Morrow instance.

Mercy was last.

"One thousand stasis modules stable," its intelligence reported. "One thousand consciousness records verified. No activation scheduled before destination safety checks."

Salvador touched the edge of his console.

"Bring them somewhere they can complain."

"That is consistent with the mission charter."

"I know. I read it."

***

Egon recorded his farewell before the corridor opened.

He did not address the body as if it could hear him.

He addressed the person who might one day watch.

"You will remember this room," he said. "You will remember agreeing to go. I am recording this after the final map, so you won't remember saying these words."

His own face watched him from the preview window.

"I don't know whether you'll want our name. You can keep it. You can choose another.

"You don't have to finish my life."

He looked toward the greenhouse door.

Helen was waiting there with the others.

"I hope we made the beginning of yours possible."

He stopped the recording.

Then Mercy left Earth.

The display showed California becoming a brown curve beside the Pacific.

Egon remained in California, sitting before the display.

That was the separation.

No transfer of a soul that anyone could measure.

No private escape for the man who had designed the mission.

A living person watched a ship carry the possibility of another.

***

For almost six years, Mercy crossed the shortened dark.

The vessel was never empty.

It was never awake in the human sense.

Morrow inspected the bodies, maintained the protected records, tended the farms, and moved through the ship in machines Mateo and Leena had helped design.

The propulsion system advanced along the same time reference as Earth. No return to an earlier date was permitted.

A narrow communications channel followed the compressed route. While it remained open, Earth could send observations, technical advice, and personal messages. When Mercy closed the corridor at Redo, ordinary radio would again need thirty-five years to cross the intervening distance.

Morrow stored the distinction in every arrival briefing.

It also stored messages from parents who understood the distinction and sent birthday greetings anyway.

***

On November 18, 2177, coolant pressure fell behind the stasis decks.

Morrow traced the leak to a conduit hidden beneath a shielding mount.

Two inspection models had missed the defect.

The intelligence quarantined both models and recorded the failure.

The remaining loop could sustain six hundred and eighty bodies.

One thousand traveled aboard Mercy.

There was no human crew to wake.

Morrow transmitted an emergency request to Earth and began the procedures already authorized for this situation.

The ecological vault possessed an independent cooling loop.

Diverting it would preserve the dormant settlers and destroy much of the living collection.

The directive had been argued over before launch.

Its final text was short.

Preserve the people who may awaken. Record what is lost. Do not pretend the loss is nothing.

Morrow began moving viable samples into the active farm.

Robotic hands lifted trays in a room where nobody could hear the alarms.

The source team had approved this emergency directive before launch. Morrow completed the diversion without waiting for Earth's reply.

All one thousand bodies stabilized. The machines began assessing what could still be saved in the damaged vault.

***

Sanya's delayed reply reached the ship through the corridor.

Her face was streaked with dust. Earth had been having a worse day than the dispatch record explained.

"The dry seed packets can survive without the vault," she said. "Move the living cultures with the lowest storage tolerance first. Don't waste time trying to save an inventory entry that already failed viability."

She had only the initial fault packet and its proposed diversion, not the later confirmation that the work was done.

Then she looked directly at the camera.

"Do it."

Her dormant counterpart lay several decks away.

She did not hear the decision.

She would inherit its consequences.

***

Morrow kept all one thousand bodies stable throughout the repair.

The repairs took thirty-one Earth days, ending on December 19, 2177.

Sixty-one percent of the living cultures were lost.

The dry poppy seeds survived.

The intelligence added the loss to the arrival record, alongside the names of the source teams who had collected each sample.

It did not label the event a perfect rescue.

On December 22, delayed relay packets began arriving with collision warnings dated December 21.

Then most of them stopped.
