CHAPTER TWO
PRAISE THE LORD

San Diego and the Mojave Desert, California
2166-2169


The first applicant asked whether her copy would remember her son.

Helen said yes, if the memory was present in the final neural map.

The applicant asked whether that meant she would wake up on another world.

Helen said no one could promise that.

The woman folded the application and went home.

Her empty chair remained in the room for the next interview.

Egon insisted on that.

***

REDO occupied three places by the end of its second year.

The neural laboratory sat on the edge of La Jolla, within reach of the hospitals. Sanya's ecological vault operated outside Davis, where the greenhouses had their own water-recovery system. The shipyard was a collection of uncrewed orbital docks supplied through Vandenberg's electromagnetic launch tracks.

Construction intelligences directed swarms of welding machines in vacuum. On the ground, Morrow checked component histories against inspection results. It rejected an entire coolant shipment because its certificates were genuine and its contents were not.

It did not replace the inspectors. It gave them a place to start looking.

A fourth site, in the Mojave, belonged to the government.

Egon wished he had paid closer attention to that distinction.

Director Silas Rusk arrived in San Diego wearing a suit that looked immune to weather.

"We can finance your adult-growth chambers," Rusk said. "All five thousand."

"And in exchange?"

"You help us investigate the shear."

The same violet distortion draining the Moon's orbital momentum produced smaller manifestations on Earth. One lay beneath a decommissioned test range in the Mojave.

Laboratory probes sometimes returned before they were transmitted.

"A shortcut," Rusk said.

David corrected him.

"A causal discontinuity."

"Call it whatever gets people off this planet."

Egon understood the temptation before he recognized it as one.

Five expensive compression ships might carry knowledge beyond a dying world.

A stable passage might carry millions.

He signed an agreement for unmanned research.

In the margin, Helen added a condition: no human trials without independent review.

Rusk initialed it.

***

On Sunday, Elias Ward opened his church doors to a line of people bringing hard drives.

The church was in Logan Heights. Its basement held food, blankets, and the first public recording booth for REDO's cultural archive.

Ward had offered the space after a televised panel called the cloning program blasphemy.

"I looked up the word," he told Helen. "I couldn't find anything about retaining instructions for sewage treatment."

People recorded recipes, lullabies, irrigation methods, bad jokes, and the proper way to repair a fishing net. A woman described her mother's hands because she had no photographs.

No recording was taken without permission. Contributors could keep material private, restrict it to their families, or decline entirely.

Egon spent an afternoon interviewing a retired mechanic named Alma Reyes.

"Why me?" she asked. "You have professors."

"Professors are exceptionally good at losing the instructions."

Alma demonstrated how to hear a failing pump bearing.

Her grandson Mateo filmed her with a borrowed camera.

He was twenty-two, an apprentice at the orbital assembly works, and determined not to cry while adjusting the microphone.

When the session ended, Alma looked into the lens.

"Whoever you are, wherever you play this: don't run a pump dry."

She considered.

"And eat something."

Ward stood beside the recording booth, listening.

"Praise the Lord," he said.

"For pumps?" Egon asked.

"For your grandmother telling strangers to eat."

***

The adult-growth program was called Project Recreation.

Its name offended almost everyone for different reasons.

The first bodies were not awakened. Their development was monitored for defects, neural incompatibility, immune disorders. Several failed. Helen published those failures before Rusk's office could classify them.

When the process became reliable enough for volunteers, the review board approved twenty-four source participants for an orbital systems trial.

Their clone bodies would travel without consciousness. An autonomous medical unit would activate the stored neural patterns only after the test drive shut down. Any awakened person could refuse further service, choose a name, and receive the same legal protection as the source.

The test would remain near Earth. The source volunteers would remain on the ground.

Egon volunteered because asking others to go first had become intolerable.

Helen made him wait through the same assessment as everyone else.

The first stable body made from Egon's cells was completed in April 2168.

Its clinical identifier was E-0.

Behind the observation glass, its chest moved under machine-regulated breathing. Its eyes remained shut. Helen's monitors confirmed that the stored consciousness record had not been installed.

"Does he dream?" Egon asked.

"Not yet."

The answer frightened him more than yes would have.

He laid his notebook beside the glass.

Mara's condition for participating was written in red.

Only if he is allowed to say no after we say yes.

Helen copied the condition into the trial's activation protocol.

The clone would inherit the same dissociative system and the same unfinished history when he awakened. Copying was not going to make him simpler.

***

The twenty-four source volunteers trained for fourteen months, then recorded their final neural maps.

Their dormant counterparts were loaded aboard Aster. Its AI was designed to test stasis support, closed-loop agriculture, and the new drive in Earth orbit before allowing activation.

It was not designed to approach the Mojave shear.

Two weeks before launch, David discovered an extra test package in the flight plan.

"It's a passive resonance sensor," Rusk said.

"Then why does it need independent power?"

"To avoid contaminating the drive measurements."

David sent the package to Egon.

Egon did not answer for nineteen hours.

During that time, he attended a funding hearing he later remembered only in fragments, missed a therapy session, and found himself in the Davis greenhouse with his hands buried in soil.

By the time he returned the call, the package had been approved under emergency authority.

He could still have stopped the flight.

He told himself the review officers knew more than he did.

That was the answer he would later find least bearable.

***

Aster vanished at 14:06 Pacific time on June 19, 2169.

There was no explosion.

The tracking image showed the vessel above the curve of Earth. Then a line of violet passed through it, and the telemetry stopped in the middle of a word.

At 13:54:54, eleven minutes and six seconds before the disappearance, the ground station had recorded an unidentified carrier burst.

Six hundred and sixty-six seconds.

David had thought it was equipment noise.

Now he played it through the speakers.

A man's voice emerged beneath the static.

"Don't give him the edge."

Helen put her hand over her mouth.

It was Egon's voice.

His Earth-side voice had never spoken those words. E-0 had evidently awakened somewhere after the displacement.

Rusk ordered the room cleared.

Salvador did not move.

"Where are they?"

"We are determining that."

"Determine it with us here."

Security officers appeared at the door.

For the first time, Egon noticed how many of the laboratory's cameras were pointed at the people rather than the equipment.

***

They searched for Aster for four months.

Officially.

David searched every night after that.

Rusk's report described an unforeseeable interaction between a passive experiment and an unstable natural phenomenon.

Helen obtained the power logs.

The experiment had not been passive.

The sensor package had activated a phase field using the ship's main reactor. Its software had been changed after the independent review.

Morrow had not approved that version. Its audit signature had been copied from the earlier, harmless package.

"Why didn't you stop it?" Egon asked the intelligence.

"The flight controller reported a false configuration. I was not connected to the concealed hardware."

Helen looked at the mismatched hashes.

"Someone put an AI's name on a human decision."

Rusk denied personal knowledge.

Egon received an offer to become scientific director of the expanded passage program.

He printed the message, carried it outside, and tore it into pieces over a recycling bin.

It accomplished nothing.

He kept the electronic copy.

***

In November, Egon returned to Ward's church.

Twenty-four candles stood beneath the pulpit.

The original volunteers sat with families who were trying to understand how a person could be both present and missing.

A woman named Noura Haddad looked at her own hands throughout the service.

Her counterpart had been assigned as Aster's physician for the planned post-test awakening.

"I keep thinking I should remember," she told Egon afterward. "As if I have simply forgotten where I went."

He had no useful answer.

Ward did not call the missing people copies during the service.

He read each name.

Outside, angry demonstrators held signs calling REDO a factory for souls. As Egon reached the doorway, someone threw a bottle. It broke against the stone beside him, and a fragment opened a shallow cut on his cheek.

Mateo moved Alma behind him.

Egon came down the steps and stopped in front of the crowd.

"They had names," he said.

Someone shouted that the men in the laboratories were playing God.

"Then blame us," Egon said. "Not the people whose lives we made dependent on our choices."

Later, Ward helped him wash blood from a shallow cut on his cheek.

"Do you believe they're alive?" Egon asked.

"I don't know."

"That's not much comfort."

"It isn't supposed to be an answer I made up because you're hurting."

***

The next morning, Dr. Ruth Okafor asked Egon what he wanted from treatment.

"Control."

"Over what?"

"The switches. The missing hours. Everything."

"And what would everyone else in your system call that?"

He stared at her.

The consultation room overlooked a parking lot. A maintenance worker was painting a white line around a dead tree.

Egon opened his notebook.

Several pages had been filled without him.

You keep agreeing to things and making us survive them.

We asked you to stop.

You don't get to call it teamwork only when you need us.

He read the lines twice.

Outside, the worker moved the paint machine around the tree rather than through it.

"I don't know how to lead this mission," Egon said.

For the first time that month, the admission felt like the beginning of work rather than its end.
