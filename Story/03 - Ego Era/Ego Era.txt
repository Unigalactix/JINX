CHAPTER THREE
EGO ERA

California
2170-2171


Egon's first successful act of leadership was canceling a meeting.

He had slept three hours. His hands were shaking. There was a message in the notebook he could not remember writing, asking him not to enter the neural laboratory that morning.

For most of his life, he would have made coffee and gone anyway.

Instead, he called Helen.

"I can't chair the review."

"I'll ask Miriam."

He waited for disappointment.

"Anything else?" she asked.

"No."

"Then go back to bed."

The review happened without him.

Nobody died.

He found this both reassuring and offensive.

***

Ruth did not offer to turn twelve identities into one obedient voice.

She helped him build continuity.

There were grounding exercises, calendars, protected sleep, written agreements, and appointments he was no longer allowed to label optional in his own head. There were difficult sessions that left him unable to do anything afterward except sit in the car while Helen drove.

Some weeks, communication improved.

Other weeks, it did not.

The notebook became an encrypted shared journal because paper could be lost, though Kit still preferred to draw in the margins of printed pages.

With explicit permission, a private medical assistant reminded him of meals, appointments, and agreements. Its records remained separate from Morrow's mission systems. Neither the government nor a shipboard intelligence was entitled to turn his treatment into a surveillance feed.

Egon.
Abel.
Mara.
Kit.
Jonah.
Inez.
Tomas.
Ada.
Lewis.
June.
Sol.
Nell.

Twelve names, not twelve convenient specialists.

They shared a brain, a body, and a history. They did not form a committee of supernatural talents. Sometimes two remembered the same event differently. Sometimes several wanted nothing more complicated than a quiet room.

Abel was willing to speak with Helen when Egon could not.

Mara insisted that exhaustion was not evidence of moral commitment.

Inez maintained the calendar until Egon stopped treating it as advice.

Tomas distrusted Rusk.

So did everyone, eventually.

Nell took months to leave a message at all.

When she did, it was only three words.

Please keep tomorrow.

Egon copied them onto the inside of his office door.

***

In August 2170, he testified before the REDO oversight board.

He disclosed the unanswered message before Aster's launch. He provided his agreement with Rusk, the altered flight plan, and the power logs Helen had obtained.

"Did your condition cause the loss?" a board member asked.

"The unauthorized phase-field activation caused the loss," Egon said. "My failure was not escalating a warning I eventually received. I was overextended, and I protected the schedule instead of the people. You should judge that decision."

Helen sat behind him.

He did not turn around to see whether she approved.

The board suspended human passage experiments.

It also refused to hand him unrestricted control of REDO.

He remained scientific lead under a new command structure. Flight safety belonged to Salvador. Medical decisions belonged to Helen. Environmental release decisions required Sanya's approval. Miriam chaired an independent launch panel.

Any one of them could stop an operation.

Rusk's office called the arrangement cumbersome.

Salvador framed the complaint.

***

The newspapers called 2171 the beginning of the Ego Era.

Egon hated the phrase until Mateo printed it on a mug and filled the mug with dead pens.

"Now it's useful," Mateo said.

The ships had names by then.

Mercy would go to Redo.

Sequoia would travel to Tau Ceti.

Pacific would seek a settlement around Epsilon Eridani.

Lantern was bound for Barnard's Star.

Peregrine would approach 61 Cygni.

Four destinations were nearer than Redo, but none had offered a guarantee of a habitable surface. The settlers would carry the means to build sealed orbital homes.

Three unmanned compression freighters had now completed qualification runs. Their independent clocks agreed with the forward-only navigation model. Every settlement ship would carry its own Morrow instance, isolated from the others and physically unable to override the chronology interlock.

The drive could shorten distance.

It was not allowed to shorten yesterday.

Sanya refused a publicity request to replace that explanation with the phrase new Earths.

"We are going to worlds," she said. "We don't get to promise they want us."

The same thousand consenting source volunteers would have a dormant counterpart on every ship. Their consciousness records would remain inactive until their destinations were reached. Selection balanced essential work, languages, cultures, ages at neural mapping, and a lottery among qualified applicants. It was imperfect, argued over in public, and barred from accepting purchased places.

The source volunteers trained on Earth so their final maps could carry the practical skills of settlement. No source person would board an interstellar ship. No conscious fleet clone would endure the compression field.

Morrow would survey each destination, establish safe receiving conditions, and only then begin awakening.

The fleet also carried a diverse, consented reproductive bank. Future generations would not be limited to the genetic pool of a thousand settlers.

Morrow simulated shortages and published the failures rather than a single flattering survival score. The engineers nicknamed it the jinx.

"A prediction does not cause a coolant leak," it said.

"Neither does saying the word quiet," Salvador answered. "We still don't say it during a landing."

When the nickname escaped into the news, the whole project acquired it.

JINX.

Egon let the press keep the word. He had begun to distrust names that promised good fortune.

Cloning was only the beginning of a population.

It was not supposed to become its caste system.

The archive expanded beyond anything Egon had imagined.

Navajo engineers in Arizona contributed maintenance manuals in two languages. A Louisiana cooperative sent flood records and music. Nurses in Oakland recorded how to calm a frightened patient when the medicine cabinet was empty.

People also sent propaganda, grudges, and lies.

The archivists preserved provenance and disagreement rather than pretending knowledge arrived clean.

One evening, Egon found Sanya labeling a packet of ordinary California poppy seeds.

"We have thousands of those," he said.

"These came from a vacant lot where my mother used to wait for the bus."

She sealed the packet.

"The others don't replace them."

***

David came to the greenhouse with a discovery and the expression of a man who wished he had made a different one.

He had reconstructed Aster's last field measurements.

The unauthorized equipment had included a narrow experimental cutting tool. Engineers called it an edge, a stabilized boundary in the phase field intended to separate material without friction.

It had been listed as a sample collector.

The equipment photograph showed a grip and a line of metal so thin it almost vanished.

Egon felt the chair beneath him become distant.

He named five things he could see.

Pot. Window. David. Door. Blue notebook.

Helen, arriving with coffee, stopped talking.

"Do you need a break?"

"I need to tell you something."

For months, Egon had dreamed of a plain covered with white dust.

He had assumed it belonged to his own difficult history transformed by sleep.

But there were things in it he had never known: a city called Orun, a woman with a broken tooth, a king who wore a ring shaped like a closed eye.

And a sword.

David placed the photograph flat on the table.

"These are not coming from your childhood."

"How do you know?"

He opened another image.

In 2163, an archaeological team had recovered a fractured ceramic panel near the remains of an inland sea. The damaged inscription described the destruction of an otherwise obscure city. The central figure carried a narrow weapon.

Beside the figure were twenty-three marks.

"I looked for the waveform geometry," David said. "Not your dreams. This came up in a materials database because something embedded in the panel couldn't be dated."

Helen leaned closer.

The embedded fragment was manufactured polymer.

Its surviving print read AST.

***

They took the evidence to Miriam.

She ordered independent tests before anyone was allowed to say time travel.

The tests took seventeen days.

On the eighteenth, she locked the conference room.

"The panel is ancient. The polymer is from Aster's insulation batch."

Salvador stared at the table.

"They're in the past."

"They were in the past," David said carefully. "We don't know when they died."

Egon looked at the photograph of the sword.

Rusk had stopped sending messages through assistants.

Now he called personally, asking what they had learned about the returning signal.

Miriam moved copies of the evidence outside his department.

***

The first red tide appeared off Torrey Pines in October.

Marine biologists initially suspected an algal bloom.

Then the samples came back containing human blood.

The beaches closed.

The news blamed an industrial accident.

Egon stood at a laboratory sink, staring at a sealed specimen tube.

Helen waited while the medical database completed a match against the Aster volunteers.

Noura Haddad's identification appeared on the screen.

Then a second contributor.

Then a third.

Blood from the missing.

Blood from strangers who were not in any database.

All arriving in the same cold water.

David called from the Mojave station.

"The shore anomaly is building. It peaks tomorrow at sunset."

"What happens at the peak?"

"I don't know."

Egon took out the shared journal.

He did not ask for permission to disappear into a danger nobody else understood.

He explained where he wanted to go, why, who would be with him, and how they would leave if he became overwhelmed.

Abel answered first.

We go with them. Not instead of them.

Egon called Helen back.

"I'm going to the coast."

"I know," she said. "I'm driving."
