CHAPTER FOUR
666

Torrey Pines, San Diego
October 2171


The ocean withdrew before the rain began.

Not far. Perhaps six feet along the beach.

But it moved uphill.

Water climbed the seaward faces of the rocks and held there, trembling like skin above a pulse.

Salvador checked the anchor twice.

"I dislike everything about this."

Egon stood in a safety harness at the foot of a sharp-edged rock below the sandstone cliffs. Helen secured the tether to his belt. Farther inland, Sanya watched the sampling instruments inside a field shelter.

David's voice came through their headsets from the monitoring van.

"The pulse is opening. Estimated duration: six hundred and sixty-six seconds."

They had closed the access trail and cleared the beach. Harbor crews were keeping boats outside the exclusion zone.

The precautions made Egon feel briefly foolish.

Then the first drop struck his hand.

Red.

Warm.

He looked up.

The clouds above the Pacific had acquired a dark seam. Along it, the sunset bent in two different directions.

Another drop struck the rock.

Then hundreds.

Blood fell through the cold evening, stippling the sand, darkening the sleeves of Helen's jacket.

"Confirmed," Sanya said. Her voice was thin. "Hemoglobin. Human cellular material."

Egon climbed onto the rock.

Its edge bit through the sole of his boot. He steadied himself with one hand and looked out over the ocean.

The rain thickened.

Red ran down the stone in narrow streams. Drops burst against the black water and spread into branching clouds beneath its surface.

The smell reached him.

Iron. Salt. Something old released from a sealed room.

Then the sea was gone.

***

A short spear.

A boy under a wagon.

An empty palm raised from a riverbed.

Egon felt the weight of a blade in his right hand.

Not a dream. Not one of the ordinary discontinuities he had spent years learning to navigate.

There was another man's memory forced against his own.

He saw a mouth form the words You won.

His hand moved.

The rain struck harder.

He was on the rock again, gripping the tether.

"Egon." Helen's voice. "Look at me."

He tried.

Behind her face, the king's platform flashed into existence.

Twenty-three prisoners.

Noura with a broken tooth.

His own face reflected in a clean blade.

"It's him," he said.

"Your counterpart?"

"Yes."

David spoke over a burst of interference.

"The field is coupling to the neural-map carrier from the original transfer. Your implant is acting as a receiver. We're shutting its wireless channel."

The implanted interface had been fitted for the mapping work. It was not supposed to receive anything without consent.

Another assumption the shear had made dangerous.

Helen activated the hardware isolation switch.

The visions stopped.

Egon dropped to his knees on the rock.

Blood ran over his hands.

Around them, the rain continued.

***

"We need to leave," Salvador said.

Egon looked toward the horizon.

Within the dark seam, something was rising.

A sheet of red lifted from a landscape that could not be seen from California. For an instant, a white plain lay above the ocean, inverted beneath the clouds.

Blood rose from its ground as if the afternoon were being unwound.

It gathered into the seam.

Then it fell over San Diego.

"We're going backward," Egon whispered.

"What?"

"Not forward. The passage. Rusk hasn't found a road to the stars."

The white plain flickered.

Salvador pulled the tether.

"Down. Now."

Egon climbed off the rock as a crack opened through its summit.

They ran for the shelter.

Behind them, a section of stone slid apart so smoothly that it made no sound until it hit the water.

***

The rain ended after eleven minutes and six seconds.

The beach remained red.

Coast Guard crews expanded the closure. Hospitals prepared decontamination stations. Sanya refused to let anyone dismiss the samples as harmless because their source was ancient.

Inside the monitoring van, Egon sat wrapped in a thermal blanket.

"Blood doesn't evaporate into intact cells and rain down," Helen said.

He nodded.

"That was what it looked like."

David replayed the instrument data.

The anomaly was transporting matter, not ordinary water vapor. Its entry surface had opened over the ancient plain. At the California end, the transported material condensed into droplets inside a storm front.

The field's visual distortions made the collection look like evaporation in reverse.

There was nothing natural about it.

"Why here?" Sanya asked.

David displayed the coastline.

A violet point pulsed beneath a Navy installation inland from Point Loma.

"Because someone built a receiver."

***

Rusk called before they could call him.

"I understand you've had a difficult evening."

Miriam joined the connection from Sacramento.

"You have an active human-passage experiment beneath San Diego."

"I have a potential evacuation route."

"The board suspended those experiments."

"The board has not provided additional places in the Earth refuges."

Egon leaned toward the microphone.

"We saw where Aster went."

Silence.

Then Rusk said, "Come to the installation. All of you. You should see what we're trying to save."

***

The receiver occupied a room beneath sixty feet of concrete.

Its central chamber resembled a dry swimming pool surrounded by coils. Within it, a vertical sheet of dim light shivered against the air.

Beside the chamber stood a sealed display case.

Inside lay a narrow blade.

No rust. No blood.

Egon stopped walking.

"Where did you get that?"

"It came through the receiver three weeks ago."

"And you told nobody?"

"It contained the data we needed to locate Aster's exit."

Rusk touched the case with two fingers.

"The hilt is a field recorder. Its owner kept it powered until the cell failed."

Helen stared at him.

"You've had their last words for three weeks."

"I've been trying to make those words not be their last."

***

They listened in a small room with no windows.

The recording began with the sound of breathing.

Then Egon's voice.

Older than the Earth-side Egon's voice. In the full recording, they would find the same warning fragment the ground station had received before Aster vanished.

"My name is Egon Stein. Mission identifier E-0. We arrived more than six years ago. Our vessel broke on landing. Its medical unit activated us when the field shut down. Twenty-four of us woke on an Earth none of our maps recognized. We mistook survival for safety."

He described the king's soldiers, the confiscated equipment, the failed attempts to escape.

The phase edge had remained locked for years. He had believed keeping it disabled would prevent the king from understanding its use.

Then fever reached the prisoners.

Noura needed access to the ship's medical stores. Rusk's field package had fused their compartment shut. Egon reactivated the edge to open it.

The king saw what it could do.

"He offered release for all of us if I broke Orun's resistance. I told myself the soldiers would attack and I would frighten them into surrender."

There was a long silence.

"I had already seen what the blade did."

Helen set both hands flat on the table.

"There were ten thousand," the voice said. "A man tried to scratch my hand because I had promised him that would be enough. Tarek. I learned his name when his brother called it from beneath a wagon."

Egon lowered his head.

Abel's words came through the shared body, quiet and clear.

"Keep playing it."

The recording continued.

"The king didn't release the others. I knew before I climbed those steps that he wouldn't. I finished anyway because stopping would have meant admitting what the earlier deaths had purchased."

A cough.

"I will not call this another part of me. We remember what we did. It was a choice."

The room was utterly still.

The source Egon felt sick with recognition, but recognition was not memory. He had not stood on that plain. Another living person, carrying his starting history, had done so.

He owed that distinction to the dead as much as to himself.

The recording resumed.

"Noura made me help her get the others out that night. We took the edge back to the platform. The king died with it against his throat. It did not change the plain."

The escape had cost lives.

Nine captives had been killed before they reached Aster's wreck. Others died in the following winter. The recorder contained names, burial directions, and Noura's account correcting details Egon had tried to soften.

She had survived long enough to record the correction.

"Don't build him a statue," she said. "Don't turn what happened to us into an excuse for what he did."

Near the end, Egon's counterpart described an intermittent resonance from the damaged field package.

He had placed the edge beside the damaged package, with the recorder still running inside its hilt. The next return pulse would carry the whole weapon away.

"If somebody finds this, don't give him the edge. Whoever is offering you a better world in exchange for obedience, don't give him the thing that makes refusal impossible."

The final minutes contained only breathing.

Then they did not.

***

Rusk waited outside.

"Now you understand why we need to go back."

Helen looked at him with open disbelief.

"To rescue them?"

"If the exit can be adjusted."

David shook his head.

"You don't control the exit date. You found a fixed connection."

"We control it better every day."

"You just rained a massacre over San Diego."

Rusk's expression changed.

Not anger.

The patience of a man who believed other people kept interrupting necessity.

"There is a habitable Earth on the other side. Billions of empty acres."

"Not empty," Sanya said.

"Less populated."

Egon understood.

The rescue was the acceptable beginning of the sentence.

The rest was conquest.

"You'll send engineers," he said. "Soldiers. Administrators."

"People who can establish order."

"A king with better equipment."

Rusk stepped closer.

"Your five ships save five thousand copies. I might save fifty million people."

Egon had spent years preparing for arguments against his science.

He had not prepared for an argument that was, in part, true.

Fifty million people.

Real people who wanted mornings.

Helen spoke first.

"Can you move them without increasing the lunar shear?"

Rusk said nothing.

David turned his tablet around.

He had overlaid the receiver's power cycles with lunar measurements.

Each activation had increased the loss of orbital momentum.

The natural shear had started the disaster. Rusk's attempt to exploit it was accelerating the deadline.

"Every opening moves the collision closer," David said.

"Temporarily."

"Lost momentum doesn't come back when you stop drawing the graph."

Miriam's voice came from the doorway.

"This installation is closed."

She held a federal safety order.

Behind her stood military police who did not report to Rusk.

***

The receiver took forty hours to shut down.

Morrow supervised the reduction from a physically separate controller while human engineers verified each stage. Its forecasts showed several quicker closures.

All of them risked tearing open the connection.

This time, nobody selected the quickest answer.

Closing it stopped the additional loss.

It did not restore the Moon's orbit.

The interstellar seed fleet still had to leave before the safe launch window ended. For the original people, there was no new escape route. They would face the crash on Earth, inside whatever refuges could be finished.

Rusk was relieved of authority while investigators examined the altered flight software and the concealed deaths. His supporters called him the only man willing to attempt a real rescue.

Egon did not dismiss all of them as fools.

Some had children without refuge places.

He knew what a promise sounded like to someone in a locked room.

The blade went into independent custody.

The massacre recording went into the public archive with the victims' names wherever those names were known.

Egon insisted on including the boy under the wagon.

Unknown was not the same as unimportant.

***

Three nights later, Helen found him at the coast above the closed beach.

The water below was dark again.

"Do you think we would have done it?" he asked.

She stood beside him.

"I think asking whether you are secretly a monster can become another way to avoid asking what you will do next."

He watched the surf.

"I approved that mission."

"Yes."

"I wanted the shortcut."

"Yes."

"I still want a world where everyone gets out."

"So do I."

For a while, they said nothing.

Then Egon opened the shared journal.

There were messages waiting.

No one had asked him to stop being afraid.

Mara had written:

We don't have to become him to remember him.

Below it, in Kit's uneven lettering:

Can we grow something tomorrow?

Egon looked at the ocean until he could answer.

Yes.
