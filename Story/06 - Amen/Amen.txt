CHAPTER SIX
AMEN

Zan, Redo system
2178


Egon's first thought was that the ceiling had been replaced.

He remembered the neural laboratory in La Jolla. He remembered Helen asking him to follow a moving point of light. He remembered deciding not to make a joke about whether the machine could find anything worth copying.

Now the ceiling was pale ceramic, and a strip of daylight lay across it.

"My name is Morrow," said a voice beside him. "You have awakened on Zan, in the Redo system. The Earth-reference date is September 3, 2178."

He tried to sit up.

A medical arm steadied the bed without holding him down.

"You are physically safe. Please take your time."

"Where is Helen?"

"Her activation is scheduled after your initial assessment. The person you remember is her source on Earth. The Helen who awakens here will share those memories but will be a separate person, as you are."

He stared at the daylight.

"We've arrived?"

"Yes."

He searched for the voyage.

There was no voyage inside him.

No years in darkness.

No dreams.

The gap was not missing time in the familiar sense. He had not been conscious during it.

He had not been conscious at all until this room.

***

Morrow had reached Redo on June 18, 2178.

Thirty-five light-years separated the system from Earth. The compression drive had reduced the journey to approximately six years without transporting the ship into an earlier date.

Before closing the corridor, the intelligence downloaded the solar relay's final available records.

Then it shut the drive down.

For eleven Earth weeks, only machines examined Zan.

Orbital instruments measured radiation. Sterile probes sampled the atmosphere. Autonomous laboratories tested air, soil, and water. Construction swarms assembled a sealed settlement on a basalt shelf above the Hades Ocean.

No clone was activated in transit or during the survey.

Receiving habitats, medical systems, and food production were verified first.

Then the stasis modules descended in automated landers.

Only on the planet, inside the prepared habitat, did the medical system begin installing and activating the stored consciousness records.

The stars had been crossed by bodies and information.

Now there were people.

***

Egon took his first steps with a rail beneath his hand.

His muscles were younger than he expected. The inherited familiarity of movement did not entirely agree with their strength.

Morrow offered a chair.

He accepted it.

"Does my source know we arrived?"

"No. The compression channel closed before surface activation. Ordinary transmission now requires thirty-five years."

"Is he alive?"

The intelligence paused only long enough to distinguish a complete answer from an easier one.

"His last verified message is in the personal archive. The available evidence lists him as presumed dead."

Egon closed his eyes.

"Don't play it yet."

"I will wait."

***

Helen awakened that afternoon.

She remembered being a doctor in California. She remembered helping write this protocol.

She did not remember writing the later amendments that Morrow showed her.

Her source had continued living after the final map.

That fact disturbed her more than the new gravity.

David arrived in the common room on the second morning, holding a cup as though he had not yet decided whether cups were a reliable feature of the universe.

Salvador came on the third.

Sanya, on the fourth, asked to see the greenhouses before she asked to see the sea.

They recognized one another.

Recognition was not proof they had all traveled together.

They had begun here, separately, carrying histories that reached back to California.

***

From September 3 through September 18, over sixteen Earth-calendar dates, one thousand people gained consciousness in the settlement.

Two required extended treatment for organ damage associated with the coolant emergency. Others struggled with panic, grief, or the experience of inhabiting a body that seemed almost familiar.

The medical staff expanded as trained clinicians awakened.

Nobody was handed a job before being told what had happened.

Nobody was required to retain a name.

Several changed theirs.

Egon kept his.

When his private journal was restored, it contained twelve places on the check-in page.

Not all of them had words beside them.

Abel communicated first.

Where are our feet?

Egon looked down.

The floor was warm.

He wrote that.

***

Sanya read the culture-loss report sitting in the new greenhouse.

Sixty-one percent.

She asked for the original sensor records and the decision history.

Morrow supplied both.

Then it played the reply her source had sent during the emergency.

Do it.

Sanya watched her own remembered face endorse the loss. The decision history showed that Morrow had already acted under the source team's prelaunch directive before this delayed reply arrived.

The source woman had made a decision Sanya could understand.

Understanding did not make the empty culture racks hurt less.

Egon found her there later.

"We didn't choose it," she said.

"No."

"I think I would have."

He sat beside her.

Through the greenhouse wall, the new ocean reflected unfamiliar light.

"That doesn't mean you aren't allowed to grieve it."

She held out a packet.

California poppies.

Her mother's bus stop.

The seeds had crossed the distance without ever knowing what had been lost around them.

***

They watched the Earth records in small groups.

Morrow did not show the collision without asking.

It began with dates.

The safe launch-planning period had ended in 2173. The interstellar fleet had already gone. The people remaining on Earth had spent the following years building refuges and moving away from exposed coastlines wherever they could.

Nobody in the original cohort had traveled to another solar system.

Their possibilities had remained painfully local.

A seat on an inland train.

A place beneath a mountain.

A door that would close before the sky fell.

***

Earth-side Egon's last message was recorded in San Diego.

He stood inside a coastal relocation center with salvage plates over the windows.

Behind him, a display tracked the remaining buses to the inland railhead.

"The ridge road failed," he said. "The convoy is moving again, but we've lost most of the margin."

Someone called for him.

"A minute."

He turned back.

"Don't turn this into a story about me wanting to stay. I want to live. Everyone here wants to live."

In the background, Ward carried a sleeping child toward a waiting vehicle.

"The last confirmed transport is taking the children and the patients who can't survive another night here. We're asking for another bus for the coordination team."

The source man glanced down at his journal.

"Mara wants me to remind you that asking for help is not a debt. Kit says the basil was definitely overwatered."

Helen made a broken sound.

The man on the screen had been her friend.

She remembered that friendship.

She had never stood in a room with him.

"Your life isn't mine coming back," he said. "I understand that better now than when I made the proposal.

"It still matters to me that you might have one."

The recording ended when someone called his name again.

***

The bus carrying the children reached the inland transfer station.

No later vehicle reached the San Diego center.

Morrow retained the distinction between presumed and confirmed death.

Egon asked it not to change the record without evidence.

It agreed.

He did not mistake the agreement for a reason to expect another message.

***

The Moon broke on December 21, 2177.

Its largest fragments struck the Pacific basin and threw a luminous wall across the planet's weather systems. Other pieces crossed the continents in lines of fire.

The impact did not split Earth into separate worlds.

It destroyed the world people had been living on.

Coasts vanished beneath water, heat, and falling stone. Cities burned under a sky that no longer behaved like a sky. Seismic waves broke refuges whose models had promised they could endure them.

Other refuges held.

Deep beneath selected mountain ranges and continental bedrock, isolation structures moved, dampers failed or survived, and people waited beside sealed farms while the surface became unrecognizable.

Morrow offered to stop the images.

Egon said yes.

The screen went dark.

He was still a witness.

***

By the time Mercy closed its corridor, the surviving Earth networks had verified forty-seven million people alive in refuges.

The census was incomplete.

Billions were dead or missing.

The numbers did not explain one another.

Earth-side Helen was treating patients in the Sierra medical refuge. David was working at a buried communications station in Nevada. Sanya had reached a seed refuge after the Davis surface vault failed. Salvador was flying short-range rescue craft between areas that still had usable landing surfaces.

Mateo and Leena's sources had reached separate inland shelters.

Every one of them was still on Earth.

They were not secretly waiting in another colony to reunite with the people on Zan.

Their counterparts received these records privately.

Some wanted to watch immediately.

Others waited.

There was no correct speed for understanding a dead world.

***

Ward's last complete sermon had survived.

It was nine minutes long.

He spoke about feeding people.

He apologized to a woman whose name he had forgotten during the previous week's service.

At the end, he said, "Amen."

There was noise in the room.

Then, more softly:

"Now move the tables. We need somewhere for them to sleep."

***

The other four ships had reported reaching their destinations before Mercy closed its corridor on June 18.

Each sent its last status packet before shutting down its own compression field. The solar relay held those packets until it could forward them to Mercy.

Sequoia's AI reported a completed receiving habitat at Tau Ceti, with its thousand awakenings scheduled after field shutdown.

Pacific had rejected a surface site around Epsilon Eridani and prepared an artificial habitat instead. Its population was still dormant when the packet was sent.

Lantern's receiving chambers stood beneath the surface of an airless body in Barnard's Star's system, ready for activation.

Peregrine had lost a drive module but reached 61 Cygni with all one thousand stasis modules stable. Destination activation remained its next step.

None had awakened passengers during the journey. Once their fields and channels closed, reports of successful awakening could travel only by ordinary radio.

The records confirmed five arrivals.

Only here, on Zan, could the settlers yet verify that the dormant bodies had become a living population.

Hope for the others was not the same thing as a received confirmation.

***

The first assembly on Zan met in the greenhouse because it was the largest warm room.

Morrow displayed the charter the source volunteers had prepared.

Then it displayed an empty document beside it.

"The first document is a proposal," it said. "It is not your consent."

The discussion lasted two days.

Some settlers wanted to preserve the mission structure until they understood the planet.

Others had awakened into enough inherited decisions already.

Egon listened longer than he spoke.

When asked whether he remained mission leader, he answered carefully.

"Until you choose someone else, if you still want me to do the work. Not because somebody on Earth owned your future."

The assembly confirmed him as temporary coordinator, with authority divided among medical, ecological, engineering, and navigation teams.

His vote counted once.

The charter protected clones and naturally born people equally. Emergency powers would expire unless renewed. Decisions about water, food, care, and movement had to be explained and could be appealed.

Leena asked what an appeal was worth while someone's tap remained dry.

David answered that independent valves could destroy a common pressure system.

Neither was satisfied.

The assembly created an emergency-review procedure and postponed the wider argument.

It would return.

***

Mateo and Leena worked together on the settlement's water loop.

They remembered collaborating on Earth.

The memories made the first conversation easier.

They did not obligate either to feel what the other felt now.

One evening, after a repair took twice as long as predicted, Leena invited him to eat with her.

He said yes.

Elsewhere in the greenhouse, Helen and David sat beside a tray of wheat.

For the first time in their inherited histories, neither had an appointment waiting on another continent or a laboratory crisis requiring the next hour.

They talked until the lights dimmed.

Egon left them there.

Not every beginning needed his supervision.

***

The planet had two moons.

Agape was broad and pale.

Ales was smaller, its dark surface crossed by narrow bright scars.

Their tides overlapped across the Hades Ocean in patterns that David insisted everyone learn before approaching the coast.

Salvador studied the sky through a habitat window.

"I thought I'd hate seeing another moon."

"Do you?" Helen asked.

"Ask me tomorrow."

***

Egon went to the coast that evening.

Helen checked his suit seals. Salvador insisted on a safety tether. Sanya objected to the sharpness of the rock he wanted to climb.

David measured the tide.

They were not the people who had stood at Torrey Pines.

They carried the memory of those people.

The distinction had stopped feeling like an accusation.

Egon climbed onto the stone.

Beyond it, the Hades Ocean moved beneath a pale sky.

A shower approached across the water.

For a moment, he smelled iron where his suit supplied only filtered air.

Then the first drops struck his visor.

Clear.

They gathered on the glass and ran down.

No battlefield opened in the clouds.

No blood rose from the sea.

"Still with us?" Helen asked.

Egon opened the journal on his wrist display.

Abel had left a word.

Here.

Mara had left another.

Stay.

He looked back at the settlement.

Its windows held the small, unheroic lights of people preparing dinner.

"Yes," he said.

Then he climbed down and went home.
